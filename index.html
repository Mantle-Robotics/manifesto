Autonomy kits for the machines already in the mine.

Underground mining works almost the same way it did fifty years ago. The machines got bigger and went electric, but there is still a person at the face running them. We're building retrofit kits that make the jumbo drills mines already own run on their own. After the drill, we'll do the same for every other machine in the drill and blast cycle, until nobody has to be underground.

Manifesto

Robots are for doing work

Robotics right now is in its demo era. Humanoids fold laundry on stage, and every few weeks there's a new model picking cups up off a table. The videos look great. Very little of it is producing anything.

We think the goal of robotics is to eliminate human labour. A robot is a tool, and a tool is worth what it produces. So the question we ask about any robot is simple: how much work does it get done with no one there?

Mines are already full of machines that do real work. Jumbos, bolters, scoops, charging rigs. They're proven, they're paid for, and they were built to survive underground. The one thing they can't do is run without someone in the seat or on the remote. That's the part we build. We don't need to invent a new robot for the mine. The robots are already down there. We just have to let them work by themselves.

Zero people in the tunnel

At The Boring Company the rule was zero people in the tunnel. Two of us worked there, next to ex-miners. The rule sounds like a safety policy, but it's really an engineering constraint. If no one is allowed in, you have to go through every single task and figure out how a machine does it. What you end up with is faster and cheaper than the old process, because it was never designed around what a person can reach, see, or get through in a shift.

We want to bring that rule to mining: zero people in the mine.

Zero people at the face. Then zero people in the heading. Then zero people underground.

How we get there

Start with the drill

Mines aren't going to scrap working jumbos to buy new autonomous ones, and most of them couldn't afford to. So we start with what's already there. We add the sensors, perception, and boom control to the rig the mine already runs, and it drills the pattern on its own.

Then the rest of the cycle

Drilling is one step. The full cycle is drill, charge, blast, ventilate, muck, scale, bolt, and repeat. Once the drill runs itself, we automate the other machines in the cycle. Then we build the software that runs all of them together. It knows the state of every heading, sees where the cycle is about to stall, and sends in the right machine. At that point the mine doesn't need anyone underground.

The problem

Mines have to produce more metal with fewer people

Demand is going up

EVs, grid batteries, transmission lines, wind turbines, and the data centres running AI all need copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, and rare earths. You can't write software to replace a tonne of copper. The IEA expects lithium demand to grow fivefold by 2040 and projects a 30 percent shortfall in copper supply by 2035 under current policies. Closing that gap takes roughly $500 billion in new mining investment, and a lot of the metal is in hard rock orebodies that you can only reach underground.

The workforce is going down

In the US alone, the Society for Mining, Metallurgy & Exploration expects 221,000 mining workers to retire by 2029. That's more than half the country's mining workforce. Fewer people are coming in than leaving, and the ones leaving take decades of experience with them that was never written down anywhere.

Why not just hire more people?

This is the obvious answer, and it doesn't work. Underground mining is remote and dangerous, and mines are competing for the same small pool of people. Even when a mine does find someone, it takes years to train them to run a jumbo well, and the people who would train them are the ones retiring.

Canada is in the same spot. The Mining Industry Human Resources Council expects the Canadian industry to need about 191,000 hires between 2024 and 2034, and says that by 2034 close to a quarter of them will be hard to fill.

Opening new mines doesn't fix it either. In the US it takes 29 years on average to go from discovery to production, and in Canada it takes 27. Every new mine also needs its own crew. Bigger machines move more rock per hour, but they still need an operator. Every option that keeps a person in the loop runs into the same wall: there aren't enough people.

The only way to grow what a mine produces without growing the number of people it needs underground is machines that work without people. That's why we think automation is the only real solution.

The easy ore is gone

The deposits that are left are deeper, lower grade, and harder to find. In 1997 the industry made 18 major copper discoveries. Since 2010 most years have had fewer than five, and 2023 had none. The ore that does get mined holds less metal: in Chile, the world's largest copper producer, the average grade fell by about a third in just over a decade. Lower grade means moving more rock for every tonne of copper, and deeper deposits mean more of that rock comes from underground.

Underground is where people get hurt

Most people killed in underground metal mines are killed by the work itself: driving and working around mobile equipment, or standing under ground that hasn't been supported yet. Those are exactly the jobs in the drill and blast cycle. Every task a machine takes over at the face is one less person in the places where people die.

Productivity has gone backwards

Since 1995, manufacturing more than doubled its labour productivity and agriculture grew by about half. Mining's dropped by half. McKinsey puts it down to deeper mines, longer hauls, lower grades, and a decade with almost no new technology, and says the industry can't meet demand without a major change in how it works.

The face hasn't changed

Sandvik and Epiroc do sell automated drilling, and on a new top-end rig it works. But most small and mid-tier mines run older machines that don't have it. On those rigs, a person still stands at the face, lines up the boom by eye, drills the pattern, charges the holes, backs out, and waits for the blast.

What operators told us

We've talked to more than a dozen people who run or have run underground operations. The automation Sandvik and Epiroc sell comes with a new multi-million dollar machine. That works for a big mine buying a new fleet. It doesn't work for a mid-tier mine running rigs it bought ten years ago.

Most of the focus is on the surface

Most of the autonomy work in mining so far has gone into the surface: haul trucks on open-pit ramps and drill rigs on flat benches. Those are easier problems, with GPS, open sky, and room to move. Underground has had much less attention.

Why a retrofit

If autonomy only comes with a new machine, most mines won't get it this decade. A kit that fits the rigs already on site is the fastest way to get it into the mines that actually need it.

Every kit also collects data. Each hole we drill tells us more about the rock, the rig, and the cycle. And because we don't sell equipment, we can go on every brand of machine at the site. The equipment makers can't do that.

Goals

What we're doing, in order

  • First
    Drill a pattern with no one at the face

    Our kit on a real jumbo at a real test mine, drilling a full pattern with the operator out of the heading.

  • Then
    Run one full cycle

    Drill, charge, blast, muck, scale, and bolt, with a machine doing each step and a person only supervising.

  • Eventually
    Run a heading with nobody underground

    Software that tracks every heading, catches stalls before they happen, and sends the fleet through the cycle on its own.

What we've built

At The Boring Company's Not-a-Boring Competition, we won the navigation challenge with an autonomous rover that drove itself through a tunnel with no GPS. The next year we came back with Canada's first student-built micro tunnel boring machine.

Starting a design team from nothing is a lot like starting a company. We had a $40,000 budget and ten months. We recruited the best engineers we knew at Waterloo and grew the team to 30 students, all of us building it while in school or on internships. In those ten months we went from an empty CAD file to a two-tonne machine with 100,000 lbs of thrust and six times the torque of a BMW M4, cutting through dirt in Texas. That's how we plan to build Mantle.
